Pros and cons

Pulled directly from the full reviews - see each car's page for every individual quote and rating.

Hyundai Creta (Petrol/Diesel)

Pros
  • Three genuinely well-sorted engine options for different buyers, all confirmed refined and dependable by long-term testers
  • 6 airbags and a Level 2 ADAS that works reliably on real Indian roads, confirmed independently by multiple reviewers
  • Cheapest real running cost of any engine option if you pick the diesel or NA petrol sensibly
  • Over 1.2 million sold - strong resale value and dealer network confidence
Cons
  • No wireless Android Auto/CarPlay despite the price - the single most-repeated complaint across every review
  • Turbo-petrol DCT's real-world city mileage (7-9 kmpl) is genuinely poor, worsened by E20 fuel
  • Suspension and brake-noise niggles reported by more than one long-term owner
  • Noticeably fewer features than the closely related Kia Seltos at similar money

Hyundai Creta Electric

Pros
  • Real running cost of roughly ₹1/km on home charging - about one-eighth of the petrol automatic's ₹8/km
  • Genuinely quick (8.1s 0-100 tested) and noticeably quieter and more composed than even the ICE Creta
  • Early reliability signal is strong - a real 70,000km owner reports zero brake pad wear and no measurable range loss
  • 8-year/1,60,000km battery warranty is a real safety net most rivals don't match
Cons
  • A real ₹2-5 lakh premium over a comparable ICE Creta that more than one reviewer calls genuinely hard to justify
  • Real-world range (350-430km) consistently falls short of the claimed 390/473km figures
  • Exposed, low-hanging battery pack is a real (if so-far-unrealised) damage risk on bad roads
  • Routine service cost isn't dramatically cheaper than the ICE car's, despite having fewer wear items

Our verdict: which should you buy?

There's no single winner here - it genuinely depends on your usage

Both cars land at an identical 7.8/10 in our aggregation, and that's not a coincidence - it reflects what the real reviews actually show. The Electric is the better car to drive and the cheaper car to run, full stop. But it asks for a real premium upfront, and whether that premium makes sense depends entirely on how much you drive and whether you can charge at home.

Autocar India's own long-term math puts the breakeven at roughly 28,000km with home charging - call it 18-24 months for an average Indian driver, sooner if you drive more. If you'll comfortably clear that distance and have a place to plug in overnight, the Electric's running-cost advantage compounds in your favour for as long as you own the car. If you drive under 10,000km a year, don't have reliable home charging, or simply want the lowest sticker price and widest service network, the regular Creta - ideally the diesel or the naturally-aspirated petrol, not the thirsty turbo - remains a genuinely sound, well-reviewed choice.

Pick the ICE Creta if
You drive under 10,000km/year, or can't charge at home

The diesel's real 20+ kmpl highway mileage and the NA petrol's low running cost make more sense than paying an EV premium you'll never earn back. Avoid the turbo-petrol DCT unless performance genuinely matters more than fuel bills to you.

Pick the Creta Electric if
You drive 15,000km+/year and can charge at home

You'll clear the price premium inside 2 years on fuel savings alone, and you get a genuinely quicker, quieter, more composed car in the meantime - backed by the strongest early reliability signal of any new EV we've covered.

Is the EV actually "greener"? The honest answer

Both "EV = zero emissions" and "petrol is fine, EVs are a scam" are oversimplifications. Here's what the actual lifecycle research says, specifically for India.

Petrol and diesel cars are not clean. Every litre burned releases CO2, along with real local-air-quality pollutants - particulate matter, NOx, and visible smoke, especially from diesel engines under load - that directly affect air quality in the cities where these cars are actually driven. This is well documented and isn't in serious dispute.

But an EV in India is not "zero emissions" either - it's cleaner, by a real but moderate margin. The electricity that charges a Creta Electric overnight comes from a grid that is still roughly 70-74% coal-powered as of early 2026. That matters, because unlike the exhaust pipe, the emissions just happen somewhere else - at the power plant instead of your street.

In other words: the EV genuinely does come out ahead over its full life, even on India's coal-heavy grid - independent studies consistently find real lifecycle savings, not a wash. But the margin is nowhere near the "zero emissions" framing used in advertising, and a bigger share of the EV's footprint is front-loaded into manufacturing the battery before the car has driven a single kilometre. As India's grid adds more solar and wind, that advantage will grow - it's already improving year over year - but it isn't there yet.

There's a real cost on the EV side beyond electricity, too. Battery production is resource- and energy-intensive, and India's battery recycling infrastructure is still immature, meaning end-of-life handling is a genuine open question for cars like this reaching the 8-10 year mark. Neither side of this comparison is environmentally free - one burns fuel in front of you, the other shifts most of its footprint to a factory and a power plant you never see.

Our honest take: if lower lifecycle emissions genuinely matter to your decision, the Creta Electric is the better choice on the numbers - but buy it for the running cost and the driving experience first, and treat "it's basically carbon-neutral" as marketing, not fact. And if you're sticking with the ICE Creta, the diesel and NA petrol's better real-world fuel economy also means less burned per kilometre than the thirsty turbo - which matters for your fuel bill and for what comes out of the tailpipe.
